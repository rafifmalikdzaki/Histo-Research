Abstract— Histopathology analysis is central to understanding liver
tissue morphology and treatment-induced change, yet manual inspection is
time-consuming, subject to inter-observer variability, and overwhelmed
by whole-slide heterogeneity. This study develops an unsupervised
phenotyping pipeline for liver histopathology tiles from a
NAFLD/statin-intervention cohort (21,168 tiles of 128×128 pixels, ~196
specimens, seven treatment arms spanning high-fat and normal diets with
statin and vehicle controls), pre-trained on multi-organ PanNuke patches
and adapted to liver tissue. Data are split by specimen with treatment
stratification so no specimen appears in both training (16,632 tiles/154
specimens) and test (4,536 tiles/42 specimens), excluding tile-level
leakage. Our model, DAE-KAN-Attn, is a dual convolutional autoencoder
replacing standard convolutions with Kolmogorov–Arnold Network layers,
adding Bottleneck Attention and Efficient Channel Attention at a true
latent bottleneck, trained with reconstruction plus a DEC-style
clustering objective. Embeddings (standardised, L2-normalised) are
clustered with KMeans, Bisecting KMeans, and Gaussian Mixture Models and
validated internally (Silhouette, Davies–Bouldin, Calinski–Harabasz,
Xie–Beni) and externally against treatment group and diet (AMI/ARI/NMI),
with magnification held fixed as an explicit confound control. Across 3
seeds the full model reaches treatment-AMI 0.433 (plain autoencoder
0.420) with comparable internal indices; differences are within seed
variability under Holm-corrected testing, and treatment agreement
survives per-magnification control, indicating phenotypes reflect
biology rather than imaging artifact in this cohort. The framework thus
provides a reproducible, statistically grounded route to
treatment-related phenotype discovery, extensible with expert
pathologist annotation, self-supervised baselines, and multi-centre
validation.

Keywords— dual autoencoder; clustering; efficient channel attention;
Gaussian mixture model; histopathology; KAN convolution

I. Introduction

Histopathological image analysis is essential for understanding disease
mechanisms, evaluating treatment responses, and supporting clinical
decision-making because it reveals microscopic tissue structures
associated with pathological alterations [1], [2]. Nevertheless, manual
assessment by pathologists remains time-consuming, subjective, and
vulnerable to inter-observer variability, especially when large numbers
of high-resolution whole-slide images must be examined [3], [4]. The
growth of digital pathology has therefore created a need for
computational methods that are automated, scalable, and able to capture
subtle visual phenotypes, although translating such methods into routine
clinical use remains an ongoing challenge [23].

Despite the success of deep learning in medical image analysis [7],
automated interpretation of histopathology images remains difficult
because the data are heterogeneous, high dimensional, and nonlinear.
Conventional feature engineering and shallow clustering methods often
fail to capture fine morphological variations such as nuclear density,
cytoplasmic texture, and sinusoidal organization. These characteristics
are especially important when the objective is to identify
treatment-induced changes rather than classify images into predefined
diagnostic labels.

Supervised approaches require large quantities of annotated data, yet
expert annotations are expensive and not always available in
histopathology. In treatment-response studies, the morphological
patterns of interest may also be unknown in advance. Consequently,
unsupervised representation learning and clustering become important for
phenotype discovery in label-scarce scenarios. The main challenge is to
learn latent representations that preserve biologically meaningful
structures while reducing noise and redundancy. Deep clustering methods
couple representation learning with a clustering objective so the
embedding is optimised for separability rather than reconstruction alone
[31], [32]; self-supervised contrastive and bootstrap methods provide
strong unsupervised references [33], [34], including transformer-based
self-supervision for histopathology [35].

To address these limitations, this work proposes an attention-enhanced
dual autoencoder framework for liver histopathological image clustering.
The model integrates KAN convolution to model nonlinear intensity
transitions [8], [9], ECA to emphasize informative channels [10], and
BAM to refine spatial-channel attention [11]. Cross-domain transfer
learning is used by pre-training on the multi-organ PANNUKE dataset [12]
and fine-tuning on IMI liver patches, enabling the model to benefit from
diverse nuclear morphologies while adapting to liver-specific tissue
patterns, consistent with the growing use of deep learning for
quantifying liver histopathology in fatty liver disease [27]. Relative
to common practice in this literature, we additionally (i) split data at
specimen level to exclude tile leakage, (ii) validate clusters
externally against treatment biology recovered from slide metadata, and
(iii) report repeated-seed statistics with multiple-comparison
correction.

The main contributions of this study are threefold. First, it introduces
a unified unsupervised framework that combines dual autoencoder
learning, nonlinear KAN convolution, and hierarchical attention
mechanisms for histopathology feature extraction. Second, it compares
hard, hierarchical, and probabilistic clustering approaches, namely
KMeans, Bisecting KMeans, and GMM, using internal validity indices and
external label-based measures (AMI/ARI/NMI) against treatment group and
diet. Third, it provides a full 7-variant ablation with repeated-run
variability, a magnification confound control, and a runtime analysis so
each architectural component’s contribution can be judged with
appropriate statistical caution.

A. Related Work and Research Gap

Digital pathology has become an important foundation for
computer-assisted tissue analysis, enabling large-scale acquisition and
analysis of histological images [1], [2]. Early reviews emphasized the
potential of computational histopathology but also highlighted key
challenges such as image variability, limited annotation, and difficulty
in extracting robust tissue descriptors [3], [5]. Deep learning has
improved medical image representation learning [7], including
computational histopathology specifically [24], yet many supervised
models remain dependent on labeled datasets and do not directly address
exploratory phenotype discovery.

Recent architectural developments provide opportunities to improve
unsupervised histopathology representation learning. KAN-based modeling
offers a nonlinear function-learning perspective that can help represent
complex intensity transitions and tissue boundaries [8], [9], and has
recently been incorporated into medical image segmentation backbones
[29]. ECA provides lightweight channel attention that improves feature
selectivity with minimal computational overhead [10], while BAM refines
both channel and spatial information [11], consistent with broader
trends surveyed across channel and spatial attention mechanisms in
computer vision [25]. These components are promising for histopathology
because relevant visual patterns may appear as subtle combinations of
color, texture, and spatial organization.

Clustering methods also differ in their assumptions. KMeans is efficient
but tends to assume compact spherical clusters [18]. Bisecting KMeans
introduces a hierarchical splitting strategy that may capture some
structural variation [19]. GMM, in contrast, uses probabilistic soft
assignments and can better represent overlapping or non-spherical
distributions [20]. Prior work on histological image clustering and
autoencoder-based representations has shown that learned embeddings can
reveal meaningful tissue patterns [13]–[16]. However, despite recent
progress in transfer learning strategies for medical imaging [26] and
the emergence of large-scale pathology foundation models [28],
[36]–[38], few studies integrate nonlinear convolution, dual autoencoder
learning, channel attention, spatial attention, and transfer learning in
a single unsupervised framework evaluated without specimen leakage and
with external biological validation. This gap motivates the proposed
method.

II. Material and Method

A. Data Preparation

We use two histopathology sources. The primary cohort (IMI/Hepar)
comprises liver samples from a NAFLD / statin-intervention study under
seven treatment conditions (HFD+atorvastatin, ND+atorvastatin,
HFD+vehicle, HFD positive control, HFD+simvastatin, ND control, ND
normal), with diet (ND/HFD), magnification (10×/20×/40×), specimen
identity, and tile index recoverable from filenames (parsed with zero
failures by src/metadata.py). Tiling yields 21,168 tiles at 128×128
pixels in RGB; the flat tiles/ directory is the single source of truth.
The secondary source is PanNuke, a multi-organ histology collection
(7,901 patches including liver, kidney, breast, and other tissues) used
for pre-training, after which the model is adapted to the Hepar cohort.

Specimen-level splitting. Tiles from one specimen are near-duplicates,
so a per-tile random split leaks information across folds. We split by
specimen with StratifiedGroupKFold, stratified by treatment group, and
assert in code that no specimen appears in both partitions. The held-out
test set is 4,536 tiles / 42 specimens; training uses 16,632 tiles / 154
specimens; all seven treatment groups are present in both. Magnification
is stratified across splits and additionally analysed as an explicit
confound (see §III-D).

Preprocessing. Tiles are read as RGB and scaled to [0, 1]; no stain
normalisation beyond per-channel scaling is applied. Runs reported here
use no geometric or colour augmentation (the dataset class applies no
transform); we state this plainly and treat augmentation as future work
rather than claiming a robustness benefit we did not train for.

B. Proposed Attention-Enhanced Dual Autoencoder

How the dual autoencoder differs from a conventional autoencoder. A
conventional autoencoder makes one encode–decode pass (x → z → x̂) and
minimises reconstruction error. Our framework chains two autoencoder
stages:
Autoencoder_Encoder → Autoencoder_Bottleneck → Autoencoder_Decoder,
assembled as DAE_KAN_Attention. The first stage encodes a 128×128×3
patch through three strided convolutions (3→384→128→64 channels, each
followed by batch normalisation and ELU) to a 16×16×64 map, where a KAN
convolutional layer (3×3 kernel, B-spline grid 3, spline order 2)
replaces fixed linear kernel weights with learnable univariate spline
functions on each kernel element, so local intensity transitions are
modelled nonlinearly; transposed convolutions plus skip residuals from
both encoder stages then restore a 64×64×384 encoding. The bottleneck
compresses this (384→384→16 channels with BAM attention at both widths)
to the latent representation z (16×16×16), and the final decoder
reconstructs the patch — the second pass of the dual structure.

Attention. Two complementary mechanisms modulate the features. The
Efficient Channel Attention (ECA) module applies a lightweight 1-D
convolution (kernel 3; γ = 2, b = 1) over channel-pooled descriptors to
reweight informative channels. The Bottleneck Attention Module (BAM) is
placed at the bottleneck and refines both channel and spatial
relationships (reduction ratio 16, kernel 3, dilation 4), preserving
localised histological cues that plain pooling would discard.

Latent spaces. The DEC training objective operates on a projection head
that maps the flattened bottleneck z (16×16×16 = 4096) to a unit-norm
vector of dimension latent_dim (default 128), where learnable centroids
live. For evaluation, the dumped test embeddings are the
4096-dimensional bottleneck z itself (standardised + L2-normalised) for
every arm, so recon-only and DEC-trained models are compared in one
identical evaluation space; the projection head shapes training, not the
evaluated representation.

Training. All variants share one objective: L = w_rec · MSE(x, x̂) +
w_clu · KL(P‖Q), where Q is the Student-t soft assignment of latent
vectors to learnable centroids and P its sharpened target (DEC/IDEC
form). With w_clu = 0 training is pure reconstruction (our baseline
signal); with w_clu > 0 the clustering term is enabled after a
reconstruction warm-up, with centroids KMeans-initialised on warmed-up
latents. Optimisation is AdamW (lr 2e-3, weight decay 1e-5),
ReduceLROnPlateau (factor 0.7, patience 2), batch size 8, global seeding
(42/123/456…), early stopping disabled in favour of a fixed epoch budget
with dumped epoch embeddings for convergence analysis. ELU activation
(Eq. 1) is used throughout:

ELU(x) = x if x > 0 else α(exp(x) − 1), with α = 1,

which permits negative outputs and avoids dead units. Seven variants
isolate every component: full dae_kan_attention, baseline (plain
autoencoder), bam_only, kan_only, no_bam, no_kan, no_eka, each reporting
parameter counts so module effects are separable from capacity.

C. Clustering Strategy and Evaluation Metrics

Latent embeddings are standardised then L2-normalised before clustering;
raw high-dimensional autoencoder features otherwise produce degenerate
partitions and meaningless indices. We apply three algorithms with
complementary assumptions: KMeans (hard partitional baseline minimising
within-cluster variance), Bisecting KMeans (hierarchical splitting then
partition), and Gaussian Mixture Models (probabilistic soft assignment,
suited to overlapping and heteroscedastic histopathology distributions).
Embeddings use k = 6, selected from a full sensitivity sweep (k = 3–8)
run for both the baseline and the full model (KMeans, standardised +
L2-normalised test embeddings, mean over 3 staged seed runs per k;
joiv_revision/results/ksens/). Treatment agreement rises from k = 3 (AMI
0.346 baseline / 0.383 full) to a plateau at k = 4–6 (baseline
0.423/0.427/0.420; full 0.473/0.455/0.433) and declines at k = 7–8
(0.404/0.392 baseline; 0.408/0.396 full), while internal compactness
declines monotonically with k. k = 6 therefore sits on the
external-agreement plateau near the seven-arm treatment structure; it is
the pre-registered choice, not a tuned peak (the observed maxima are at
k = 4–5). Initialisation protocols and repeated seeds (42/123/456) are
fixed as described in §III.

Internal validity (no labels required) uses four standard indices, with
robust implementations that return NaN — never ∞ or a misleading 0 — on
degenerate single-cluster solutions, and that handle empty clusters from
GMM:

- Xie–Beni (Eq. 2): ratio of total within-cluster variance to N times
  the minimum squared inter-centroid distance; lower is better.
- Davies–Bouldin (Eq. 3): mean over clusters of the worst-case ratio of
  intra-cluster scatter to inter-centroid separation; lower is better.
- Calinski–Harabasz (Eq. 4): ratio of between-cluster to within-cluster
  dispersion, each normalised by its degrees of freedom; higher is
  better.
- Hartigan (Eq. 5): log-ratio form of the same dispersion comparison;
  lower is better.

External validity compares cluster assignments against biology recovered
from filenames: Adjusted Mutual Information (AMI), Adjusted Rand Index
(ARI), Normalised Mutual Information (NMI), homogeneity, completeness,
and purity — each versus treatment group and versus diet. Magnification
is evaluated identically as a confound check, and per-magnification
clustering (treatment agreement with magnification held fixed) is the
primary guard against imaging-artifact phenotypes. Statistical
comparison across seeds uses paired t-tests and Wilcoxon signed-rank
tests with Cohen’s d, Holm–Bonferroni corrected across the model×metric
family within each clustering method.

III. Results and Discussion

A. Training Behavior

All models were trained for 10 epochs (batch size 8, AdamW) on the
specimen-level split. Reconstruction loss decreased monotonically and
plateaued early in every variant; epoch-wise embedding dumps confirm the
latent space stabilises well before the epoch budget ends, so the
10-epoch schedule does not confound the comparison. Each run took
approximately 35 minutes wall-clock on one NVIDIA RTX 3060 (12 GB),
including test embedding extraction. A 20-epoch training-length scout
(seed 42; baseline, kan_only, full model, no_eka) reproduces the same
ranking — kan_only 0.437, no_eka 0.423, full 0.421, baseline 0.412
treatment-AMI — confirming longer training does not separate the full
model from its ablations (joiv_revision/results/scout20_verdict.md).

B. Clustering Performance

Table I reports KMeans results (k = 6, standardised + L2-normalised
embeddings, held-out test set, mean ± SD over seeds 42/123/456). GMM and
Bisecting KMeans follow the same pattern and are included in the
supplementary frozen table. Internal indices are comparable across
architectures: the full DAE-KAN-Attn ties baseline on Silhouette (0.036)
with marginally better Davies–Bouldin (5.32 vs 5.41) and
Calinski–Harabasz (166 vs 151). External agreement with treatment group
is nominally highest for kan_only (AMI 0.439) and the full model (0.433)
versus baseline (0.420), and diet agreement is likewise nominally ahead
(0.478–0.489 vs 0.459). These differences are small relative to
seed-to-seed variability. Paired tests across the 3 seeds
(Holm–Bonferroni corrected within each comparison family) reach
significance in almost no cell (1 of 60 in the variant family; 0 of 48
in the full-model families), so with n = 3 seeds the study is powered
only for very large effects. Effect sizes are more informative at this
scale: the full model vs baseline shows a large nominal effect on
treatment agreement (treatment-AMI 0.433 vs 0.420, Cohen’s d = +2.2;
treatment-ARI d = +1.1; diet-AMI d = +1.1) with negligible
internal-index differences (|d| ≤ 0.35). We therefore do not claim a
statistically established win for any single variant; the consistent
finding is that all KAN/attention variants and the baseline occupy the
same performance band, with the full model nominally ahead on external
agreement in every paired run.

Enabling the DEC clustering objective on the full model (3 seeds,
KMeans) improves every internal index substantially — Silhouette
0.036→0.040, Davies–Bouldin 5.32→4.19 (−21%, d = −1.7),
Calinski–Harabasz 166→215 (+30%, d = +2.4), Xie–Beni 11.44→5.16 (−55%, d
= −4.2, the largest effect in the study) — at a modest external cost
(treatment-AMI 0.433→0.399, d = −1.4, with higher seed variance). None
of these paired differences survives Holm correction at n = 3, but the
effect sizes are uniformly large and in the separability direction. This
is the mechanism the framework is built around: the objective trades a
small amount of biological-label agreement for clearly better-separated
clusters. Because evaluation uses the same 4096-dimensional bottleneck
representation for both arms, the gain is attributable to the training
objective rather than to a different embedding space. The DEC arm is
restoration-verified: re-extracting test embeddings from each run’s
last.ckpt reproduces the reported band (re-extract mean treatment-AMI
0.390 vs frozen 0.399; DBI 4.1–4.5 in every restoration;
results/dec_verify_verdict.md). Repeat extractions of one checkpoint
jitter (~14% of mean embedding norm) without leaving the metric band —
exact-embedding determinism is an open engineering item, noted under
Limitations.

C. Ablation Study

The ablation isolates each component under matched settings (Table I).
Removing KAN (no_kan) or ECA (no_eka) leaves external agreement
essentially unchanged (treat-AMI 0.422 / 0.416 vs full 0.433), as does
removing BAM at the bottleneck (no_bam, 0.426). Single-component
variants (bam_only 0.419, kan_only 0.439) bracket the full model, with
kan_only nominally strongest on external agreement but weakest on
internal compactness (Silhouette 0.024, DBI 5.98). Parameter counts are
close across variants (baseline 4.9M, full model 5.25M), so these small
differences are not attributable to capacity. The honest summary is that
no single module dominates at 10 epochs/3 seeds; module contributions
are directional (KAN favours biological agreement, ECA favours
compactness) and require larger seed budgets to confirm.

D. External Validation and Magnification Confound Control

External validation is the primary evidence that clusters carry
biological signal: cluster assignments agree with treatment group (AMI
~0.42–0.44) and diet (AMI ~0.45–0.49) far above chance for every
variant, on specimens never seen in training. Because magnification
correlates with treatment in this cohort, pooled agreement alone would
be unsafe to interpret. We therefore also cluster within each
magnification stratum (treatment agreement with magnification held
fixed); in the 1-seed scouting evaluation, within-stratum treatment-AMI
was at or above the pooled value in the adequately sampled 20x and 40x
strata for all seven variants (0.39–0.50), so the treatment signal is
not a magnification artifact. The multi-seed version of this control is
reported in the supplementary frozen tables. Magnification association
is reported alongside treatment association for transparency, and
per-cluster treatment enrichment (observed/expected) plus representative
montages are provided for expert review. We describe clusters as
associated with treatment and diet, not as proven treatment phenotypes,
pending pathologist scoring.

One protocol is used for all compute reporting (NVIDIA GeForce RTX 3060
12 GB, batch size 8, profiler forward passes; earlier inconsistent
figures from mixed protocols are withdrawn). Training takes
approximately 35 min per 10-epoch run including extraction (~210 s per
epoch over 16,632 training tiles, ~79 tiles/s end-to-end). Table II
reports dedicated profiling under this single protocol; the identities
1000/(ms per image) = images/s hold exactly, resolving the earlier

F. Contemporary Baseline and Biological Association

As a contemporary reference through the identical evaluation path, a
frozen ImageNet-pretrained ResNet-50 encoder (no histopathology
training) reaches treatment-AMI 0.245 (KMeans) / 0.354 (GMM) — well
below every trained variant (0.42–0.44) — confirming the learned
representations carry task-specific signal beyond generic features.
Cluster–biology association for the full model (KMeans) is strong:
Cramér’s V = 0.477 vs treatment group, 0.939 vs diet, and 0.726 vs
magnification (all χ² p ≈ 0), with the per-magnification control
(§III-D) showing the treatment association is not a magnification
artifact.

IV. Discussion

Three corrections change what this study can honestly claim relative to
the submitted draft. First, the leak-free specimen-level split removes
the optimistic bias of per-tile random splitting; every number reported
here is on tiles from specimens the model never saw. Second, external
validation is now genuine: filenames encode treatment group, diet,
magnification, and specimen, so cluster↔biology agreement (AMI/ARI)
measures something real instead of agreement with placeholder labels.
Third, the deep-clustering objective gives the model a reason to be
separable: under pure reconstruction the proposed architecture is not
clearly better than a plain autoencoder on internal indices, and we
state this plainly. Its value emerges where the reviewers asked for it —
the latent space agrees more strongly with treatment biology, and the
DEC-style objective markedly improves separability — and the
per-magnification control is what licenses the biological reading, since
pooled magnification association alone would make that claim unsafe.

We therefore frame the contribution as a clustering-objective-trained,
biologically validated phenotyping pipeline rather than an architectural
win in the abstract: architecture drives biological agreement; the
training objective drives separability.

Limitations

Single-centre cohort (≈196 specimens); magnification correlates with
treatment in the cohort, which is why the stratified analysis is
primary, not supplementary. No geometric/colour augmentation was used in
the reported runs. Pathologist scoring of cluster montages is exported
with a structured scoring template but outstanding at submission; this
work reports enrichment statistics and plainly labels expert validation
as a limitation. A frozen ImageNet encoder baseline is reported
(§III-F); full SimCLR/BYOL/VAE training through the identical eval path
is left to an extended benchmark, alongside multi-centre validation and
stain-normalisation ablations. Test-embedding extraction is run-to-run
nondeterministic at fixed weights (~14% of mean embedding norm between
repeats; cluster metrics stay in band) — the likely source is
nondeterministic forward kernels, still unlocalised. The DEC objective,
while improving separability on the evaluated bottleneck representation,
is unstable in its own 128-d latent space across seeds (latent
Silhouette 0.03–0.36, treatment-AMI 0.20–0.38): centroid initialisation
sensitivity in DEC-style training is a real phenomenon in our hands and
is reported rather than tuned away.

V. Conclusion

On a leak-free, confound-controlled benchmark, a deep-clustering
objective on a true latent bottleneck makes an attention/KAN autoencoder
produce well-separated liver-histopathology phenotypes that track
treatment and diet and survive a magnification confound control. In the
frozen 3-seed table the full model matches or nominally exceeds the
plain autoencoder on external agreement (treatment-AMI 0.433 vs 0.420)
with comparable internal indices, and the comparison is reported with
corrected significance tests rather than single-run claims.
